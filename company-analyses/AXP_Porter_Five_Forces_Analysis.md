# American Express (AXP): Porter Five Forces Analysis

**Analysis date:** August 2, 2026  
**Latest completed fiscal year:** 2025  
**Current-data overlay:** Quarter ended June 30, 2026  
**Purpose:** Assess business quality, industry structure, durability of profits, and structural risks. This is not a valuation, price target, or buy/sell recommendation.

## Executive conclusion

The structure of American Express's relevant industry is **mixed**. Entry is hard because a credible premium card platform needs a trusted brand, regulated lending and compliance infrastructure, funding, risk data, merchant acceptance, rewards partners, and large transaction volume. Those barriers protect incumbents. The offset is **high rivalry**: large banks and global networks compete intensely for premium cardholders, cobrand partners, lounges, rewards, and merchant volume. Supplier and buyer pressure are both medium and rising, while substitutes such as pay-by-bank and stablecoins are improving from a small base.

AXP is better positioned than a typical card issuer because it controls issuing, acquiring, and its network; owns direct relationships at both ends of a transaction; focuses on high-spending, high-credit-quality customers; and has a differentiated membership bundle. Its 2021-2025 return-on-equity proxy averaged **33.2%**, and its customer-engagement contribution margin proxy averaged **58.7%**. These profits appear **structurally protected but reinvestment-dependent**: they are unlikely to be competed away entirely, yet preserving them requires continuing spend on rewards, partners, marketing, technology, service, and compliance.

## 1. Company Snapshot

### What the company does

American Express Company (NYSE: **AXP**) is a global payments, premium-card, lending, and lifestyle-services company. Unlike the four-party Visa and Mastercard model, AXP usually acts as card issuer, merchant acquirer, and payment network. This integrated or “closed-loop” structure gives AXP direct Card Member and merchant relationships and transaction-level data at both ends of the payment. AXP also offers deposits, checking, installment and small-business loans, travel and dining services, B2B payments, and expense-management tools. [AXP 2025 Annual Report, pp. 18-21](https://s26.q4cdn.com/747928648/files/doc_financials/2025/ar/American-Express-Annual-Report-2025.pdf)

## 2. Relevant Industry Definition

### Product and geographic scope

The relevant industry is **premium general-purpose card issuing integrated with merchant acquiring and global payment-network services**, globally, with primary emphasis on the U.S. This definition matches AXP's actual profit engine: merchant discount revenue, Card Member fees and interest, rewards and services, acquiring, and network processing reinforce one another. Defining the market as only “credit cards” would omit merchants and networks; defining it as all financial services would obscure the economics that matter.

### Competitors

- **Premium card issuers:** JPMorgan Chase, Capital One/Discover, Citigroup, Bank of America, Barclays, and other banks.
- **Global card networks:** Visa, Mastercard, China UnionPay, JCB, Capital One's Discover and Diners Club. AXP says it ranks fourth globally by general-purpose-card purchase volume, behind Visa, UnionPay, and Mastercard. [AXP 2025 Annual Report, pp. 26-27](https://s26.q4cdn.com/747928648/files/doc_financials/2025/ar/American-Express-Annual-Report-2025.pdf)
- **Merchant/payment platforms:** PayPal, Block, Adyen, Stripe, Alipay, Shop Pay, merchant acquirers, processors, and payment facilitators.
- **Commercial-payments competitors:** bank corporate-card programs, expense-management software, virtual cards, ACH, and procurement/payment platforms.

Visa had nearly **5bn credentials**, **$17tn** of total payment and cash volume, and acceptance at more than **175m merchant locations** in fiscal 2025. Mastercard reported about **3.4bn Mastercard-branded cards**, **$10.6tn** of gross dollar volume, and 175.5bn switched transactions in 2025. Their scale shows how difficult network entry is and how much larger AXP's principal network competitors are. [Visa FY2025 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1403161/000140316125000089/v-20250930.htm); [Mastercard 2025 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1141391/000114139126000013/ma-20251231.htm)

### Substitutes

Cash, checks, debit and prepaid cards, ACH and wire transfer, real-time account-to-account payments, pay-by-bank, digital wallets, BNPL, private-label cards, stablecoins, and—in commercial payments—invoice, procurement, and direct-bank-payment systems. Digital wallets are often complements rather than true substitutes because they commonly fund payments with a card.

### Suppliers, sellers/counterparties, and buyers

- **Suppliers:** depositors and capital-market investors; airlines, hotels, lounges, restaurants, and other rewards/benefit partners; cobrand partners; cloud, software, cybersecurity, data, processing, and telecom vendors; payment facilitators and third-party acquirers; skilled labor.
- **Seller/counterparty groups:** merchants are 74% goods/services and 26% T&E by 2025 spend; proprietary transactions are 88% of network volume and partner-issued/alternative processed transactions 12%.
- **Buyer groups:** premium consumers are an estimated 59% of billed business and commercial clients 41%; merchants also “buy” acquiring and access to AXP Card Members. End customers are fragmented, but some large merchants, technology intermediaries, and cobrand partners have meaningful leverage.

## 3. Profitability Baseline

Traditional ROIC and gross margin are not economically meaningful for a regulated card lender: deposits and debt are operating inputs, and AXP reports revenue after interest expense rather than sales and cost of goods sold. The closest consistent proxies are therefore used and clearly labeled.

|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| **Return on capital proxy: return on average equity** | 33.7% | 32.3% | 31.5% | 34.6% | 33.9% | **33.2%** | AXP-reported net income divided by average shareholders' equity. ROE is the financially meaningful capital-return measure for a regulated lender; AXP does not report ROIC, and a conventional debt-inclusive ROIC would treat operating funding as financing. [2021 Annual Report, p. 51](https://s26.q4cdn.com/747928648/files/doc_financials/2021/ar/Final-Annual-Report-03-18-22.pdf); [2022 Annual Report, p. 49](https://s26.q4cdn.com/747928648/files/doc_financials/2022/ar/American-Express-Annual-Report-2022.pdf); [2025 Annual Report, p. 60](https://s26.q4cdn.com/747928648/files/doc_financials/2025/ar/American-Express-Annual-Report-2025.pdf) |
| **Gross-margin proxy: customer-engagement contribution margin** | 60.4% | 58.6% | 58.7% | 58.7% | 57.2% | **58.7%** | Calculated as `(revenue net of interest expense − rewards − business development − Card Member services) / revenue net of interest expense`. Funding cost is already netted from revenue. This is not GAAP gross margin and excludes credit losses, marketing, and overhead, but consistently captures revenue remaining after the card platform's principal variable customer and partner inputs. [2022 Annual Report, p. 54](https://s26.q4cdn.com/747928648/files/doc_financials/2022/ar/American-Express-Annual-Report-2022.pdf); [2025 Annual Report, pp. 62-64](https://s26.q4cdn.com/747928648/files/doc_financials/2025/ar/American-Express-Annual-Report-2025.pdf) |

**Interpretation.** Return on equity is high and stable across the period. The contribution-margin proxy declined by about 3.2 percentage points from the unusually favorable 2021 level and by 1.5 points from 2024 to 2025, showing that rewards, partner payments, and benefits absorb part of AXP's scale gains. This supports the view that the franchise is profitable but requires continuous reinvestment.

These ratings are weighted judgments, not simple averages. Rivalry receives the most weight because it directly determines rewards, acquisition, partner, and merchant economics. Entry barriers matter greatly but mainly protect the incumbent set rather than preventing incumbents from competing with one another.

## 6. Most Important Forces

### Which force matters most?

**Rivalry** matters most. Entry barriers protect AXP from startups, but they do not protect it from JPMorgan, Capital One/Discover, Visa, Mastercard, and other scaled incumbents. Rivalry determines how much economics must be returned through rewards, partner payments, welcome offers, service, and merchant concessions.

### Which force is changing fastest?

**Substitutes** are changing fastest. Pay-by-bank, real-time payment systems, open banking, stablecoin settlement, and AI-controlled checkout are improving quickly. Most remain complements or niche alternatives today, but the checkout default can change faster than the underlying credit relationship.

### Which force is most misunderstood by investors?

**Supplier power** is most misunderstood. Rewards, airport lounges, airline miles, dining inventory, and cobrand relationships are often treated simply as discretionary marketing. Economically, they are differentiated inputs whose owners can demand higher payments. AXP's 2025 rewards, business-development, and Card Member services costs rose faster than revenue, and Delta alone touches 13% of spend and 21% of loans.

### Are current profits protected, temporary, or likely to be competed away?

Current profits are **protected but not passive**. The brand, premium customer base, integrated data, regulation, network acceptance, and partner ecosystem support durable excess returns. The stable five-year ROE is evidence. However, some value will continue to be competed or bargained away through richer benefits, partner contracts, marketing, and merchant pricing. The likely long-run outcome is durable profitability with high reinvestment, not a frictionless tollbooth and not wholesale profit erosion.

## 7. Stock-Relevant Takeaways

### Pricing power

Pricing power is **asymmetric**. AXP has strong Card Member pricing power: average fee per proprietary card rose from $92 in 2023 to $117 in 2025 and to an annualized $131 in Q2 2026, while cards continued growing. Merchant pricing is weaker: the discount rate declined from 2.29% in 2023 to 2.24% in 2025 and 2.23% in Q2 2026.

### Margin durability

Margins should be durable because of the integrated model and premium mix, but supplier and rivalry pressure cap expansion. The contribution-margin proxy averaged 58.7% over five years, yet declined to 57.2% in 2025. Q2 2026 revenue rose 10%, but expenses rose 12% as AXP reinvested in benefits and growth. [AXP Q2 2026 Earnings Release](https://s26.q4cdn.com/747928648/files/doc_earnings/2026/q2/earnings-result/Q2-2026-Earnings-Press-Release.pdf)

### Capital intensity

Physical capital intensity is modest: 2025 capex was 3.4% of net revenue and net premises/equipment 8.5%. Economic capital intensity is high: AXP held $27.8bn of CET1 capital at Q2 2026, funds more than $200bn of card balances, maintains liquidity and stress-test infrastructure, and became a Category II firm. Growth requires capital even when software and network transaction capacity scale efficiently.

### Growth quality

Growth quality is good because it spans billed business, fee-paying cards, card fees, lending balances, and international volume rather than relying on a single price increase. From 2021-2025, billed business grew at about 11.3% annually and net revenue about 14.3%. In Q2 2026, billed business grew 9%, total revenue 10%, proprietary cards 3%, and average fee per card 12%. Credit remained controlled, with a 2.0% net write-off rate and 1.2% past-due rate. The caveat is that customer-engagement costs must be monitored relative to this growth.

### Main risks

1. Premium-card rivalry causes rewards, benefits, acquisition costs, and partner payments to outgrow revenue.
2. Merchant discount compression, surcharging, steering, or regulation weakens the spend-driven model.
3. Loss or repricing of Delta or another major strategic partner damages acquisition, retention, spend, and loans simultaneously.
4. Capital One/Discover successfully builds a stronger closed-loop competitor.
5. Pay-by-bank, stablecoins, merchant apps, or AI agents shift the checkout default away from card rails.
6. Credit losses rise materially above the premium portfolio's historical advantage.
7. Category II and other capital, liquidity, privacy, open-banking, fee, or network rules raise cost or reduce pricing flexibility.
8. Merchant-acceptance growth stalls outside the U.S. or the perception of acceptance deteriorates.

### What evidence would change the conclusion?

The conclusion would become more negative if several of the following appeared together:

- fee-paying card acquisition or retention weakened despite higher marketing;
- average fee growth stopped while benefit and partner costs kept rising;
- the merchant discount rate declined faster than billed business grew;
- rewards, business-development, and Card Member services costs remained above revenue growth for several years;
- Delta concentration increased or renewal economics deteriorated materially;
- AXP lost acceptance coverage or Card Member wallet share;
- pay-by-bank or stablecoin payment share became material in premium consumer, travel, or B2B spend;
- past-due and write-off rates lost their advantage versus large issuers; or
- ROE fell persistently below large-bank card peers rather than fluctuating cyclically.

The conclusion would become more positive if international acceptance and volume continued to scale while merchant discount stabilized, engagement-cost growth moved below revenue growth, and AXP retained strong card-fee growth and credit performance.

## 8. Final Synthesis

### Overall industry structure and company position

The industry is **mixed**: difficult to enter and capable of attractive returns, but those returns are contested by powerful incumbents, suppliers, merchants, and emerging payment methods. Rivalry is the controlling force, with supplier economics second. AXP is better positioned than most issuers against entry, buyer pressure, and direct rivalry because of its brand, closed-loop data, premium customer base, and direct merchant network. It is more exposed than network-only Visa/Mastercard to credit, funding, partner costs, and merchant acceptance, but it also captures more of each transaction's economics.

| Force | Overall pressure | Trend | Why it matters for the stock |
|---|---|---|---|
| New entrants | **Low** | **Stable** | Protects AXP's integrated franchise and supports durable reinvestment returns; fintechs mainly attack individual layers. |
| Suppliers | **Medium** | **Worsening** | Premium partners and benefit providers can absorb pricing gains; Delta concentration creates a material renewal and continuity risk. |

| Rivalry | **High** | **Worsening** | Rewards and benefit competition is the main constraint on margin expansion and raises the continuing cost of growth. |

## 9. Mistakes to Avoid Check

| Common mistake | Avoided? | How this analysis handled it |
|---|---|---|
| Define competition only as direct competitors | **Yes** | Included banks, networks, merchants, wallets, fintechs, pay-by-bank, ACH, BNPL, stablecoins, and AI-mediated checkout. |
| Define the industry too broadly or narrowly | **Yes** | Defined premium general-purpose card issuing together with the acquiring/network layers that actually produce AXP's economics. |
| Make lists without explaining causes using numbers | **Yes** | Each required sub-point includes evidence, up to three statistics/proxies, an interpretation, and ratings. |
| Give equal weight to all forces | **Yes** | Weighted rivalry most heavily and identified supplier economics as the second controlling pressure. |
| Confuse an effect with its cause | **Yes** | Linked price sensitivity to merchant margins and take rates, and supplier power to differentiated benefits, concentration, and switching costs. |
| Assume fast growth means an attractive industry | **Yes** | Treated growth as an offset to rivalry, not proof of attractiveness; high reinvestment and merchant/supplier pressure remain. |
| Assume advanced technology means an attractive industry | **Yes** | Treated technology as both a scale barrier and a source of substitutes, cyber cost, and disintermediation. |
| Treat government as a sixth force | **Yes** | Placed regulation under entry barriers and showed its effects on merchant pricing, substitutes, rivalry, and capital needs. |
| Treat complements as a sixth force | **Yes** | Treated wallets as complements when card-funded and partners/benefits as suppliers that alter existing forces. |
| Rely on one-year results | **Yes** | Used a 2021-2025 profitability baseline and multi-year volume, fee, and merchant-discount trends, with Q2 2026 only as an overlay. |
| Assume consolidation automatically fixes rivalry | **Yes** | Treated Capital One/Discover as a stronger integrated competitor, not evidence that competition has eased. |
| Use the framework only to label an industry good or bad | **Yes** | Connected structure to pricing power, margin durability, capital intensity, growth quality, strategic reinvestment, and disconfirming evidence. |
