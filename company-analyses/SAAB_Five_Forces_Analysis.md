# Saab AB (Nasdaq Stockholm: SAAB B): Porter Five Forces Analysis

- **Report date:** August 31, 2026
- **Research data cutoff:** August 31, 2026
- **Latest completed fiscal year:** 2025
- **Current-data overlay:** Six months ended June 30, 2026, plus material public announcements through August 31, 2026
- **Purpose:** Business-quality, industry-structure, and profit-durability analysis. This report is informational and is **not investment advice**, a valuation, a price target, or a buy/sell recommendation.

## Executive conclusion

Saab operates in a **mixed but structurally improving industry**. Its core markets—fighter aircraft, missiles and ground combat weapons, advanced sensors and command systems, and naval platforms—have formidable barriers: classified know-how, long development and qualification cycles, export licences, trusted sovereign relationships, large cumulative R&D, specialised labour, production infrastructure, and decades of installed-base support. Those barriers protect the incumbent set. They do not eliminate two powerful offsets: governments are concentrated, politically motivated buyers, and established national champions compete intensely for a small number of very large programmes.

Saab's position is better than its size suggests. In 2024, SIPRI ranked it only **28th** among arms producers, with **$5.55 billion** of arms revenue versus **$64.65 billion** for Lockheed Martin and **$33.79 billion** for BAE Systems. Yet Saab has credible leadership positions in ground-combat weapons, live training, camouflage, airborne early warning, surface radar, electronic warfare, and conventional submarines. The clearest current validation is GlobalEye: Canada named Saab preferred supplier in May 2026, and 11 NATO Allies announced a joint GlobalEye acquisition in July 2026. At the same time, prior Gripen losses to the F-35 in Finland and Canada show that niche technology leadership does not remove scale, alliance, interoperability, and industrial-policy disadvantages. [SIPRI Top 100, 2024](https://www.sipri.org/sites/default/files/2025-11/fs_2512_top_100_2024.pdf); [Canada GlobalEye announcement](https://www.canada.ca/en/defence-investment-agency/news/2026/05/the-government-of-canada-selects-preferred-supplier-for-airborne-early-warning-and-control-discussions.html); [NATO GlobalEye announcement](https://www.nato.int/en/news-and-events/articles/news/2026/07/07/natos-new-airborne-warning-and-control-system-is-announced); [Finland HX decision](https://www.defmin.fi/en/topical/press_releases_and_news/press_releases_archive/2021/the_lockheed_martin_f-35a_lightning_ii_is_finland_s_next_multi-role_fighter.12335.news)

The financial direction is favourable but not frictionless. From 2021 to 2025, sales doubled from **SEK 39.2 billion to SEK 79.1 billion**, reported gross margin stayed near **21–22%**, and the calculated after-tax ROIC proxy rose from **6.1% to 12.1%**. The year-end backlog reached **SEK 274.5 billion**; by June 2026 it was **SEK 317.7 billion**, about four times 2025 sales, with 76% attributable to international markets. However, 2025 Aeronautics EBIT margin was only **6.2%**, versus **18.1%** in Dynamics, and H1 2026 free cash flow was **SEK -0.6 billion** as capacity investment and working capital rose. A large backlog protects revenue visibility, not project margin. [Saab 2025 annual report](https://www.saab.com/globalassets/corporate/investor-relations/annual-report/2026/saab-ar25-eng.pdf); [Saab Q2 2026 report](https://www.saab.com/globalassets/cision/documents/2026/20260717-saab-q2-results-2026-strong-order-bookings-reinforce-market-position-en-0-5398923.pdf)

The controlling force is **buyer power**. The Swedish Defence Materiel Administration (FMV) represented **36%** of 2025 sales, defence customers represented **93%**, and large orders above SEK 1 billion represented **63%** of 2025 order intake. Demand urgency is currently shifting leverage toward suppliers, but buyers still control specifications, competition design, budgets, export/state-to-state approvals, industrial participation, milestone payments, and programme cancellation. Saab's profits appear **increasingly protected, but execution-dependent**: hard-to-copy capabilities and record demand should support value capture, while fixed-price contracts, concentrated buyers, supplier bottlenecks, and capacity expansion keep the economics contestable.

## 1. Company Snapshot

Saab AB is a Swedish defence and security company that designs, develops, integrates, manufactures, and supports systems in air, land, naval, and civil-security domains. The current operating structure comprises Aeronautics, Dynamics, Surveillance, Naval (created in Q2 2026 by combining Kockums with Naval Combat Systems), Combitech, and Corporate activities. Saab employed **27,865 FTEs** at year-end 2025 and **29,380** at June 2026. It serves customers in more than 100 countries and operates in more than 30. ## 2. Relevant Industry Definition

The relevant industry is **advanced defence systems supplied to European, NATO-aligned, and selected partner-country governments**, weighted toward combat aircraft, tactical weapons and missiles, surveillance/electronic-warfare/C4I systems, and naval platforms. It excludes most commodity military supplies and pure civilian aerospace, but includes adjacent software, autonomy, uncrewed systems, lifecycle support, and integration because those capabilities alter entry, substitution, and rivalry.

- **Product scope:** Combat-air platforms and subsystems; ground combat and missile systems; air and maritime surveillance, radar, electronic warfare and command systems; submarines, surface combatants and underwater systems; training, maintenance, upgrades and technical consulting.
- **Geographic scope:** Global tender market, but with decisive national and alliance-specific procurement, export-control, interoperability, local-content and security-of-supply constraints. Europe is the economic centre for Saab: Sweden plus the rest of Europe represented **67%** of 2025 sales.
- **Main competitors:** Lockheed Martin, RTX, Northrop Grumman, Boeing, General Dynamics, BAE Systems, Airbus Defence, Leonardo, Rheinmetall, Thales, Dassault, MBDA, Hensoldt, Naval Group, TKMS, Fincantieri, Kongsberg, Nammo, Rafael, Elbit, IAI, Hanwha and other national champions. Competition varies by programme; several firms are simultaneously suppliers, partners and competitors.
- **Main substitutes:** Extending or upgrading existing platforms; buying used equipment; leasing or pooling allied capability; foreign military aid; relying on NATO or another ally; land-based air defence instead of some fighter missions; satellites/passive sensors instead of some airborne surveillance; uncrewed and attritable systems; cyber/electronic effects; commercial off-the-shelf technology; and doing without a capability where the threat and politics allow.
- **Main suppliers:** Specialised propulsion, seekers, warheads, energetic materials, electronics, semiconductors, sensors, optics, rare-earth-containing components, airframes, castings, composites, software, cloud/IT, test equipment, skilled engineering labour, and capital. GE Aerospace is the exclusive Gripen E engine provider; Bombardier supplies the Global 6500 platform underlying GlobalEye.
- **Main buyers:** National defence ministries and procurement agencies, armed services, NATO/NSPA/NCIA, other multilateral procurement groups, and prime contractors such as Boeing and Airbus for subsystem or aerostructure work.

European industrial policy increasingly shapes distribution access: the EU's defence-industrial strategy calls for at least **40%** collaborative procurement and at least **50%** of procurement budgets to be spent on European-made products by 2030. This favours Saab as an EU producer while raising the importance of cross-border partnerships and joint buying. [European Commission defence-industrial strategy](https://commission.europa.eu/news-and-media/news/first-ever-european-defence-industrial-strategy-enhance-europes-readiness-and-security-2024-03-05_en)

SIPRI's Top 100 generated **$679 billion** of arms revenue in 2024, up **5.9%**. Saab's 0.8% share of that Top-100 pool is only a scale proxy—the product mix is not comparable—but it shows that Saab is a focused challenger among much larger primes. BAE Systems reported 2025 sales of **£30.7 billion** and backlog of **£83.6 billion**; Leonardo reported revenue of **€19.5 billion** and backlog of **€46.6 billion**; Rheinmetall reported sales of **€9.9 billion** and backlog of **€63.8 billion**. [SIPRI Top 100](https://www.sipri.org/media/press-release/2025/sipri-top-100-arms-producers-see-combined-revenues-surge-states-rush-modernize-and-expand-arsenals); [BAE Systems 2025 results](https://investors.baesystems.com/investors/article~2025-full-year-results~); [Leonardo FY2025 results](https://www.leonardo.com/documents/15646808/30043445/COM_LDO_FY2025_ENG_12_03_2026.pdf/f701e90c-cca2-8e57-9b6e-bf4c9707d9d1); [Rheinmetall 2025 results](https://www.rheinmetall.com/en/media/news-watch/news/2026/03/2026-03-11-rheinmetall-presents-annual-report-for-2025?sType=LinkedIn)

## 4. Profitability Baseline

Saab does not report ROIC. The table therefore uses a calculated **ROIC proxy = EBIT × (1 − effective tax rate) / average capital employed**. Effective tax is total tax expense divided by income before tax; average capital employed is Saab's disclosed annual denominator. This deliberately excludes financial income from the numerator and is more conservative than Saab's reported ROCE, which rose from **8.1% in 2021 to 16.5% in 2025**. Gross margin is reported gross income divided by sales. [Saab financial data](https://www.saab.com/investors/financials/financial-data); [Saab 2025 annual report, multi-year overview](https://www.saab.com/globalassets/corporate/investor-relations/annual-report/2026/saab-ar25-eng.pdf)

| Metric | 2021 | 2022 | 2023 | 2024 | 2025 | Five-year average | Notes/source |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| ROIC / return on invested capital | **6.09%** | **6.74%** | **7.84%** | **9.87%** | **12.05%** | **8.52%** | Calculated ROIC proxy as defined above from Saab's EBIT, tax, and average-capital-employed data |
| Gross margin | **20.96%** | **21.15%** | **21.82%** | **21.43%** | **21.69%** | **21.41%** | Gross income / sales; values calculated from the same five annual statements |

The key pattern is improving capital return with a nearly flat gross margin. That suggests scale, operating leverage and business mix—not a broad expansion in product-level pricing—drove much of the improvement. H1 2026 gross margin rose to **22.9%** from **22.4%**, helped by a larger Surveillance and Dynamics mix, while H1 ROCE reached **17.0%** on a rolling-12-month basis. [Saab Q2 2026 report](https://www.saab.com/globalassets/cision/documents/2026/20260717-saab-q2-results-2026-strong-order-bookings-reinforce-market-position-en-0-5398923.pdf)

## 5. Five Forces Sub-Point Analysis

### Force 1 — Threat of New Entrants

| Sub-point | Evidence + up to 3 statistics/proxies | Interpretation | Pressure | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|

**Force judgment:** New-entry pressure is **Low, but worsening at the edge**. Full-platform entry remains exceptionally difficult, while commercial software, autonomy, drones, additive manufacturing, and accelerated procurement are lowering barriers in modular subsystems. EU fast-track proposals and NATO's new industry-access initiatives could broaden the entrant pool without dissolving the barriers around certified, sovereign-scale systems. ## 7. Most Important Forces

- **Which force matters most?** Buyer power. A single Swedish customer generated 36% of sales, and one Polish submarine award represented SEK 47 billion. Governments decide not only price and volume but export structure, local workshare, technology transfer, security-of-supply, milestone cash and acceptable programme risk.
- **Which force is changing fastest?** Substitutes. Cheap drones, autonomy, commercial sensors and six-week battlefield iteration cycles are shifting mission design faster than traditional procurement. This does not make fighters, radars or submarines obsolete; it changes the optimal mix and moves value toward modular software, counter-UAS, electronic warfare and mass.
- **Which force is most misunderstood by investors?** Rivalry. Record budgets do not eliminate competition, but they do change its economics. Defence remains brutally zero-sum at the individual tender level while becoming positive-sum across the portfolio. Saab can lose a fighter campaign and still grow strongly through missiles, radar, GlobalEye and naval systems.
- **Are current profits protected, temporary, or likely to be competed away?** **Increasingly protected, but execution-dependent.** Five years of rising ROIC, stable gross margin and expanding backlog indicate more than a one-year windfall. Barriers and capacity scarcity protect the profit pool; fixed-price contracts, supplier inflation, hiring, capex and customer concentration decide how much Saab retains.

### Relative company position and final synthesis

Saab is **better positioned than a typical challenger** against entry and substitutes because it combines aircraft, weapons, sensors, command systems, naval platforms, support and emerging autonomy. Its small size encourages modular design and partnerships, while reference positions in Sweden and selected niches provide credibility. GlobalEye's 2026 selections and Dynamics' 18.1% 2025 margin show genuine differentiation and operating leverage.

Saab is **worse positioned than the largest primes against suppliers and buyers**. Its purchasing scale is far below Lockheed or BAE, the Gripen E depends on an exclusive U.S. engine, and FMV concentration is unusually high. Its sovereign Swedish base is both an advantage and a constraint: it supplies a trusted reference customer and government export backing, but Sweden cannot fund the same programme breadth or alliance installed base as the United States.

Against rivalry, Saab is **strong in niches but uneven by segment**. It can win when customers prioritise adaptability, sovereignty, local industry, lifecycle control or specialised sensor/weapon performance. It is disadvantaged when an entrenched multinational fleet, alliance network, or home-market political coalition matters more. The industry structure is therefore mixed rather than uniformly attractive: barriers and growth are favourable; buyer control, fixed-price risk and national-champion rivalry keep returns earned rather than automatic.

## 8. Stock-Relevant Takeaways

- **Pricing power:** Medium and programme-specific. Saab cannot post a catalogue price to sovereign buyers, but urgent demand, scarce capacity, differentiated niches, indexation and proven delivery improve negotiating terms. Stable 21–22% gross margin alongside rapid growth suggests disciplined but not unconstrained pricing power.
- **Margin durability:** Improving. The five-year calculated ROIC proxy rose from 6.1% to 12.1%, and H1 2026 gross margin reached 22.9%. Mix matters greatly: 2025 Dynamics margin was 18.1%, Surveillance adjusted margin 10.0%, Kockums 7.9%, and Aeronautics 6.2%. A backlog shift toward lower-margin development or naval work could dilute Group leverage.
- **Capital intensity:** High and rising. Tangible capex increased from SEK 1.2 billion in 2021 to SEK 6.1 billion in 2025; H1 2026 investments in tangible assets were SEK 3.9 billion. Inventory, contract assets, specialised labour, qualification and customer-funded milestones create substantial working-capital timing risk even with customer advances.
- **Growth quality:** Strong demand, mixed cash conversion. 2025 organic growth was 25.6%, bookings rose 74%, and June 2026 backlog reached SEK 317.7 billion. H1 2026 free cash flow was negative SEK 0.6 billion, and the SEK 24.6 billion Ukraine Gripen contract was not yet in backlog at quarter-end. Growth quality depends on milestones, fixed-price execution and disciplined ramp-up, not order value alone.
- **Ecosystem control and value capture:** No meaningful ecosystem. Saab owns critical architectures, customer relationships and lifecycle know-how, but sovereignty, standards, data, export access, propulsion, airframes, weapons and local production are shared with powerful outsiders. The moat is engineering and execution, not network effects.
- **Main risks:** Fixed-price cost overruns; delays in Gripen, T-7A, submarines or other large programmes; supplier shortages and rare-earth/export restrictions; skilled-labour scarcity; poor cash conversion during capacity expansion; Swedish/FMV concentration; a change in defence budgets or political priorities; export-licence denial; lost flagship tenders; customer-required technology transfer creating future competitors; product failure/cyber compromise; rapid substitution by cheaper autonomous systems; and valuation expectations running ahead of achievable margins (valuation itself is outside this report).
- **Evidence that would weaken the conclusion:** Gross margin below 20% for several periods; ROIC proxy reversing below 8%; material project-loss provisions; backlog growth without milestone cash; sustained Aeronautics margin below 6%; supplier delays despite capex; FMV exposure rising above 40%; GlobalEye/Gripen selections failing to become firm orders; or drones/autonomy taking budget share while Saab's relevant offerings lag.
- **Evidence that would strengthen the conclusion:** Gross margin sustained above 23%; Group EBIT margin moving into low double digits without one-offs; cumulative cash conversion above the >60% target; Aeronautics and Naval margins converging toward Surveillance; more international buyers reducing FMV concentration; supplier lead times normalising; and recurring upgrade/support revenue rising materially above the current 16% service share.

The core stock-relevant question is not whether demand exists—it clearly does. It is whether Saab can convert a roughly four-times-sales backlog into timely cash and higher returns while hiring, qualifying suppliers and building capacity faster than at any point in its recent history.

## 9. Mistakes-to-Avoid Check

Yes—the analysis explicitly avoided all 15 common mistakes:

1. Competition includes direct primes, national arsenals, alliances, aid, autonomy, upgrades and doing without—not only named peers.
2. The industry is scoped to Saab's actual advanced-defence domains and geographic procurement realities.
3. Every force judgment is tied to operating, concentration, budget, backlog, cost or peer evidence.
4. Buyer power is weighted more heavily than the other forces.
5. Buyer sensitivity is explained through programme scale, fiscal scrutiny, lifecycle cost, capability and industrial policy.
6. Fast defence growth is not treated as proof of permanently attractive economics.
7. Advanced technology is treated as both a barrier and a source of substitution/obsolescence.
8. Government affects entry, suppliers, buyers and rivalry; it is not a sixth force.
