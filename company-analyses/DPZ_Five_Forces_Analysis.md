# Domino’s Pizza, Inc. (Nasdaq: DPZ) — Porter Five Forces Analysis

**Information cutoff:** 18 July 2026  
**Financial baseline:** FY2021–FY2025; latest reported operating update: Q1 2026  
**Rating convention:** “Impact” and “overall pressure” mean pressure on **industry profitability**, not whether the fact is favorable or unfavorable to Domino’s. Evidence and statistics are facts; interpretations and ratings are analytical judgments.

## Bottom line

U.S. quick-service pizza delivery and carryout is a **structurally unattractive industry for the average store, but a much better business for the scale-leading franchisor**. End customers can change meals or pizza providers instantly, local restaurant entry is feasible, category growth is slow, food is perishable, and rivals compete heavily on value. Those conditions produce **High substitute pressure and High rivalry**.

Domino’s is better positioned than the typical operator. Its 23.3% U.S. QSR-pizza share, dense store network, more than 85% digital U.S. sales mix, proprietary operating system, 6% national advertising contribution, vertically integrated dough and distribution network, and experienced franchise base create national-scale barriers that a local entrant cannot match. The corporation also receives percentage-of-sales royalties and supply-chain revenue while franchisees fund and operate approximately 99% of stores.

The distinction matters: **consumer-level switching costs are almost zero, while franchisee-level switching costs are high**. Domino’s corporate profit pool is therefore more protected than the restaurant industry structure alone suggests. Its five-year gross margin averaged 38.6% and the defined book-capital ROIC proxy averaged 86.5%, but the latter is amplified by a negative book-equity balance created by recapitalizations and share repurchases. These are business-quality observations, not a valuation or investment recommendation.

## 1. Company snapshot

Domino’s is the world’s largest pizza company by global retail sales. It sells pizza and complementary food through delivery and carryout stores, but the listed company is principally a franchisor and vertically integrated supplier rather than a conventional restaurant owner. At Q1 2026, it had 22,322 stores in more than 90 markets and approximately 99% were independently franchised. [DPZ25] [DPZQ126]

## 2. Relevant industry definition

The relevant industry is **U.S. quick-service pizza sold primarily through delivery and carryout**, with international delivery/carryout pizza included as a secondary market because approximately half of Domino’s system sales are international. This is narrower than all restaurants but broader than national pizza chains alone: local pizzerias and alternative ordering channels materially constrain price and profit.

| Boundary | Included |
|---|---|
| Product/service scope | Ready-to-eat pizza and complementary items ordered for delivery or carryout; limited dine-in where it affects the same occasion. |
| Geographic scope | U.S. as the main competitive market; country-level international pizza markets as the second scope. Competition, regulation, prices, and franchise economics are local even though brands and technology are global. |
| Direct competitors | Pizza Hut, Little Caesars, Papa Johns, regional chains, independent pizzerias, and local delivery/carryout concepts. |
| Main substitutes | Other QSR and fast-casual meals; restaurant meals found through aggregators; grocery prepared food and frozen pizza; home cooking; meal kits; and not purchasing a restaurant meal. |
| Main suppliers | Dairy/cheese and meat processors; beverage companies; other food and packaging suppliers; restaurant and supply-chain labor; landlords; vehicle/equipment vendors; fuel and utilities; technology/payment providers. |
| Main buyer groups | Fragmented end consumers; 754 U.S. franchisees; international master franchisees; and, as intermediaries, delivery/order marketplaces that influence discovery and access. |
| Buyer proportions | U.S./international end sales were **49.4%/50.6%**. Exact delivery/carryout and first-party/aggregator shares are **Not found in public filings**; the 2023 roughly-even U.S. transaction split and 2025 85%+ digital share are the best proxies. |
| Seller/counterparty proportions | Supply-chain revenue was **60.5%** of corporate revenue and regularly served **7,800+ stores**. Supplier-level spend is **Not found in public filings**; single-source contract status is the best concentration proxy. |

The U.S. QSR-pizza category was $43.4bn in 2025. The four leaders represented about 61% of delivery spending and 51% of carryout spending; independent, regional, and local operators represented the rest. Domino’s led total QSR pizza at 23.3%, delivery at 32.9%, and carryout at 19.6%. [DPZ25]

**Force judgment:** **Medium pressure, High confidence, Stable.** Local entry remains easy because consumers can switch and delivery platforms provide reach. Aggregators have lowered distribution barriers, while Domino’s market share and system scale have strengthened.

[DPZ25] | Consumer switching cost **near $0**; Domino’s share **23.3%**; digital mix **85%+** | Functional similarity makes price and convenience powerful competitive tools despite brand differences. | **High** | **High** |
| Rivalry | Fixed costs are high and marginal costs are low | A staffed store incurs labor, rent, insurance, utilities, equipment, and technology before the next order; food is variable but an incremental pizza can contribute to fixed-cost recovery. Company-store gross margin fell in 2025. [DPZ25] | Company-store food **29.9%**; labor **31.3%**; gross margin **14.3%** | Unused labor and oven capacity encourage promotions to add volume, although food and delivery labor keep marginal cost well above zero. | **Medium** | **High** |
| Rivalry | Capacity must be expanded in large chunks | Capacity is added one relatively small store at a time, and Domino’s “fortressing” deliberately adds locations within existing markets. The company opened 1,132 and closed 356 stores in 2025. [DPZ25] | Openings **1,132**; closures **356**; net additions **776** | Granular franchised expansion avoids the large-plant oversupply problem. Local cannibalization can occur, but capacity does not arrive in huge industry-wide blocks. | **Low** | **High** |
| Rivalry | Product is perishable | A prepared pizza loses value within minutes, and unused driver, labor, and oven capacity during a shift cannot be stored. Raw-material inventory is modest, but fresh dough and food-safety requirements still create spoilage risk. [DPZ25] | Inventory **$79.2m**; inventory/revenue **1.6%**; prepared-product useful life **minutes** | Perishability and time-specific capacity create incentives to stimulate orders rather than preserve inventory for later. | **High** | **High** |
| Rivalry | Non-price competition | Firms also compete through food quality, menu innovation, brand, delivery time, store proximity, order accuracy, loyalty, app quality, and marketplace access. Domino’s invests in DOM OS/PULSE, supply consistency, product launches, and national media. [DPZ25] | Advertising **$559.5m**; digital share **85%+**; U.S. stores **7,186** | Non-price differentiation protects some pricing, but the advertising, technology, and service arms race consumes resources. | **Medium** | **High** |
| Rivalry | Zero-sum vs positive-sum rivalry | U.S. category growth was 1.4% in 2025 while Domino’s share rose from 22.5% to 23.3%. Pizza Hut system sales fell from $13.315bn in 2023 to $12.794bn in 2025; Domino’s Q1 2026 still reported order-count and share gains. [DPZ25] [YUM25] [DPZQ1] | Category growth **1.4%**; Domino’s share **+0.8pp**; Pizza Hut system sales **−3.9%** over 2023–2025 | U.S. growth is substantially share-driven and therefore close to zero-sum. International store development is more positive-sum but varies sharply by market. | **High** | **High** |

**Force judgment:** **High pressure, High confidence, Worsening.** Slow category growth, abundant competitors, low switching costs, perishable output, and increasingly explicit value competition outweigh the benefits of granular capacity and non-price differentiation. Domino’s is gaining share, but its gain is evidence of competition reallocating profit rather than evidence that rivalry is weak.

## 5. Overall force ratings

| Force | Overall pressure | Confidence | Main drivers | Trend |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| New entrants | **Medium** | **High** | Easy local entry, low customer switching costs, and open aggregator distribution versus strong “Supply-side economies,” “Incumbency advantages,” and “Expected retaliation” at national scale | **Stable** |
| Suppliers | **Medium** | **High** | High “Supplier group concentration” and contractual switching costs versus commodity pricing, alternatives, purchasing scale, and cost pass-through | **Stable** |
| Buyers | **Medium** | **High** | Low concentration and small order cost versus “Few switching costs,” consumer cash pressure, and growing intermediary influence | **Worsening** |
| Substitutes | **High** | **High** | Attractive alternative meals, virtually zero switching cost, and rapid platform/retail convenience improvements | **Worsening** |
| Rivalry | **High** | **High** | Many competitors, slow category growth, price focus, perishability, and low switching costs; only granular capacity and differentiation moderate the force | **Worsening** |

These are weighted judgments, not averages. For example, supplier concentration has several High-pressure facts, but the economically important ability to qualify alternatives, benchmark commodity prices, use Domino’s purchasing scale, and pass costs to stores prevents suppliers from controlling the whole profit pool. Rivalry remains High because customer switching, slow growth, and price-led competition directly affect every order and store, even though Domino’s is outperforming peers.

## 6. Most important forces

**Which force matters most? — Rivalry.** A Domino’s order is won against national chains, thousands of independents, other QSR brands, and increasingly transparent aggregator choices. The category’s roughly 1%–2% recent annual growth means U.S. gains largely come from competitors. Domino’s is better positioned than Pizza Hut and Papa Johns on recent share, sales growth, first-party digital strength, store-level model, and supply density, and better positioned than an independent on procurement and advertising. It is not protected from promotional spending or traffic pressure.

**Which force is changing fastest? — Substitutes.** DoorDash alone processed 3.2bn orders and $102bn of marketplace value in 2025, growing 23% and 27%. That ecosystem makes other cuisines, grocery items, convenience products, and prepared retail food as easy to discover as pizza. The risk is not that consumers permanently abandon pizza; it is that a larger set of alternatives competes for every occasion.

**Which force is most misunderstood? — Supplier power.** The filing shows alarming single-source facts, and supply chain is 60.5% of reported revenue. Yet cheese pricing is benchmark-based, Domino’s says alternatives exist, commodity changes have historically passed through to stores, supply profit is shared with participating franchisees, and dollar margin can remain stable when cheese prices move. Supplier disruption matters, but it is less likely than rivalry or substitutes to absorb long-run corporate profit.

**Are current profits protected, temporary, or likely to be competed away?** Domino’s **corporate royalty and supply-chain profits are substantially protected but require continuous reinvestment**. A 99% franchise renewal rate, ten-year agreements, entrenched operating technology, dense distribution, and franchisee-funded store capital protect the franchisor. Consumer demand and franchisee restaurant margins are less protected: value promotions, wages, insurance, delivery economics, and alternative meals can compete away store-level profit. If franchisees stop earning attractive returns, corporate royalties and supply volume eventually weaken despite contractual protection.

## 7. Stock-relevant takeaways

| Topic | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Pricing power | **Relative rather than absolute.** Domino’s can preserve value and take share because procurement, advertising, and store density support low prices. Near-zero consumer switching and widespread promotions limit pure menu-price power. The more durable power is the franchisor’s 5.5% U.S. royalty and supply-chain relationship. |
| Margin durability | **Corporate margins are more durable than store margins.** Royalties lack a cost-of-sales line, international royalties need little G&A, and supply-chain changes have historically passed through. Company-store gross margin fell to 14.3% in 2025, showing the labor/food/insurance sensitivity borne mainly by franchisees. |
| Capital intensity | **Low at corporate stores, higher in logistics, largely externalized to franchisees.** Franchisees own 99% of stores. FY2025 segment capex was $67.5m, of which $57.4m, or 85%, was supply chain. The model remains capability-intensive in technology, advertising, training, and distribution. |
| Growth quality | **High quality when driven by unit economics and transaction growth; lower quality when driven only by price or buybacks.** FY2025 global retail sales grew 5.4% excluding FX, with positive same-store sales and 776 net openings. Q1 2026 slowed to 3.4% retail-sales growth and 0.9% U.S. same-store sales, so order growth and franchisee profit matter more than nominal sales. |
| Financial structure | The defined ROIC proxy is extremely high because the operating model needs little net book capital and accumulated buybacks created negative equity. That is an economic strength but not a free balance sheet: Q1 2026 debt was about $4.88bn and leverage was 4.3×. |
| Main risks | Prolonged value war; consumer trade-down to grocery/home meals; aggregator influence over discovery and economics; weakening franchisee returns; labor, insurance, cheese, fuel, or delivery cost; food-safety/brand damage; single-source disruption; international master-franchise closures; and refinancing risk. |
| Evidence that would improve the conclusion | U.S. order growth and share gains with stable franchisee profitability; continued 99% renewal; supply-chain margin at or above its recent range; international same-store sales recovering; first-party digital engagement holding despite aggregator growth; and positive net stores without high closure clusters. |
| Evidence that would weaken the conclusion | Repeated U.S. same-store growth below category inflation; promotions raising sales but reducing franchisee profit; falling renewal or supply participation; store closures spreading beyond a few master markets; delivery platforms taking a large undisclosed share of orders; structural gross-margin decline; or refinancing at a materially higher debt-service burden. |

This analysis covers business quality and industry structure. It does not provide a buy/sell recommendation, price target, or valuation.

## 8. Mistakes-to-avoid check

| # | Common mistake | Check |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Define competition only as direct competitors | **Avoided.** Included other restaurant meals, grocery/frozen food, home cooking, meal delivery, aggregators, and doing without a restaurant purchase. |
| 2 | Define the industry too broadly or too narrowly | **Avoided.** Centered on U.S. QSR pizza delivery/carryout, with international country markets as a secondary scope and the wider meal occasion treated as substitution. |
| 3 | Make lists without explaining causes using numbers | **Avoided.** Every required sub-point includes evidence, up to three statistics/proxies, interpretation, impact, and confidence. |
| 4 | Give equal weight to every force | **Avoided.** Rivalry and substitutes are controlling; ratings are weighted rather than averaged. |
| 5 | Confuse effect with cause | **Avoided.** Price sensitivity is linked to low switching cost, food inflation, alternatives, small tickets, and household pressure. |
| 6 | Assume fast growth means an attractive industry | **Avoided.** Domino’s 5%+ system growth is separated from the U.S. category’s roughly 1%–2% growth and share reallocation. |
| 7 | Assume advanced technology means an attractive industry | **Avoided.** Domino’s technology is a company advantage, while aggregator technology also strengthens substitutes and entry. |
| 8 | Treat government as a sixth force | **Avoided.** Food and franchise regulation is analyzed through entry barriers and operating compliance. |
| 9 | Treat complements as a sixth force | **Avoided.** Aggregators, franchisees, and suppliers are analyzed through distribution, buyer, supplier, substitute, and rivalry effects. |
| 10 | Rely on one-year results | **Avoided.** Used a five-year profitability baseline, 2018–2025 category context, 2023–2025 competitor trends, and Q1 2026 only as the latest update. |
| 11 | Assume consolidation automatically fixes rivalry | **Avoided.** The pending Pizza Hut sale is treated as an ownership/commitment change, not proof that price competition will ease. |
| 12 | Use the framework only to label the industry good or bad | **Avoided.** Connected the structure to royalties, franchisee health, pricing, logistics, capital allocation, margins, growth quality, and disconfirming evidence. |

## Final synthesis

**Overall industry structure:** unattractive for an average pizza restaurant, but **mixed-to-attractive for the best scaled franchisor**.  
**Controlling forces:** rivalry and substitutes.  
**Company position:** better than peers on U.S. share, digital mix, store density, supply integration, franchisee experience, and recent system growth; still exposed to the same frictionless consumer switching and meal alternatives.  
**Profit protection:** corporate profits are more durable than store-level profits, but depend on franchisee returns remaining healthy.

| Force | Overall pressure | Trend | Why it matters for the stock |
|---|---|---|---|
| New entrants | **Medium** | **Stable** | Local restaurants can enter, but a new national value-delivery system would struggle to copy Domino’s brand, density, franchise pipeline, technology, supply chain, and advertising. |
| Suppliers | **Medium** | **Stable** | Single-source food contracts create disruption risk; scale, commodity benchmarks, alternatives, profit sharing, and pass-through protect dollar economics. |
| Buyers | **Medium** | **Worsening** | Consumers are fragmented but can switch instantly; household pressure and aggregator comparison constrain pricing and demand more value. |
| Substitutes | **High** | **Worsening** | Every meal occasion faces cheaper, healthier, or equally convenient alternatives, and platform scale is improving their accessibility. |
| Rivalry | **High** | **Worsening** | Slow category growth and value competition pressure franchisee returns; Domino’s share gains show superior position but do not make the industry calm. |
