# TSMC (NYSE: TSM) — Porter Five Forces

As of August 14, 2026. Latest operating evidence is Q2 2026; the profitability baseline uses the latest five completed fiscal years, 2021–2025.

Bottom line: leading-edge foundry manufacturing is economically harsh for an average competitor but attractive for TSMC. TSMC’s barriers to entry, scale, process yield, ecosystem, and market leadership protect profits. Concentrated suppliers, enormous reinvestment requirements, and customer concentration are the main offsets. This is a business-quality analysis, not a valuation or buy/sell recommendation.

Sources used below: [TSMC 2025 Form 20-F (“F25”)](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1046179/000162828026025362/tsm-20251231.htm), [TSMC 2025 annual report (“AR25”)](https://investor.tsmc.com/sites/ir/annual-report/2025/2025%20TSMC%20Annual%20Report.E.pdf), [TSMC Q2 2026 results (“Q2-26”)](https://investor.tsmc.com/english/encrypt/files/encrypt_file/reports/2026-07/a80d7933be643644081584087731f73b22ea5a2c/2Q26%20EarningsRelease.pdf), [TrendForce Q4 2025 foundry data (“TF”)](https://www.trendforce.com/presscenter/news/20260312-12965.html), [ASML 2025 Form 20-F (“ASML25”)](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/937966/000162828026011378/asml-20251231.htm), and [Intel 2025 Form 10-K (“INTC25”)](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/50863/000005086326000011/intc-20251227.htm).

## 1. Company Snapshot

- **Company:** Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company Limited.
- **Tickers:** NYSE: TSM; Taiwan: 2330.
- **Business:** Manufactures chips designed by fabless semiconductor companies, system companies, and IDMs. It also provides advanced packaging, testing, masks, and design-enablement services.
- **Reporting segments:** One operating segment—foundry.
- **2025 service mix:** Wafer fabrication was approximately 86% of revenue; packaging, testing, masks, design, and royalties were about 14%.
- **2025 platform mix:** HPC 58%, smartphones 29%, IoT 5%, automotive 5%, digital consumer electronics 1%, other 2%.
- **Customer-headquarters geography:** North America 75%, Asia-Pacific excluding China/Japan 9%, China 9%, Japan 4%, EMEA 3%.
- **Buyer concentration:** Largest customer 19%, second-largest 17%, top ten 78%; TSMC served 534 customers and manufactured 12,682 products.
- **Supplier/counterparty proxy:** Three unnamed suppliers represented 26%, 19%, and 10% of disclosed consolidated procurement; all other suppliers were 45%. The filing does not split this procurement by equipment, wafers, chemicals, gases, utilities, or services.
- **Main cost drivers:** Fab depreciation and utilization, equipment, silicon wafers and chemicals, skilled labor, utilities, R&D, node-ramp yield, overseas-fab costs, and foreign exchange.

The latest pulse is unusually strong: Q2 2026 revenue was US$40.2 billion, gross margin 67.7%, and advanced nodes represented 77% of wafer revenue. That quarterly margin should not automatically be treated as a new normal. [Q2-26](https://investor.tsmc.com/english/encrypt/files/encrypt_file/reports/2026-07/a80d7933be643644081584087731f73b22ea5a2c/2Q26%20EarningsRelease.pdf)

## 2. Relevant Industry Definition

- **Product scope:** Global pure-play semiconductor foundry services, emphasizing leading-edge logic—7nm and below—and advanced packaging. Mature-node production is included where it affects capacity and rivalry.
- **Geographic scope:** Global. Manufacturing is concentrated in Taiwan but increasingly includes the United States, Japan, China, and Europe.
- **Leading-edge competitors:** Samsung Foundry and Intel Foundry. SMIC competes under equipment restrictions and is generally behind at the leading edge.
- **Broader foundry competitors:** UMC, GlobalFoundries, Hua Hong, Tower, Vanguard, Nexchip, and PSMC.
- **Packaging competitors:** ASE/SPIL, Amkor, and other outsourced assembly-and-test companies.
- **Substitutes:** In-house IDM manufacturing, older or less costly process nodes, alternative foundries, chiplet/advanced-packaging architectures that reduce reliance on monolithic scaling, and occasionally FPGAs or general-purpose processors.
- **Buyers:** Fabless chip designers, system companies designing custom silicon, and IDMs outsourcing some production. The best public mix proxy is TSMC’s platform and geography breakdown above.
- **Suppliers:** ASML, Applied Materials, Lam Research, KLA and other equipment vendors; silicon-wafer, chemical, gas, photoresist and metals suppliers; EDA/IP vendors; utilities; construction firms; and specialized labor.
- **Seller/counterparty proportions:** TSMC held 70.4% of global foundry revenue in Q4 2025 versus Samsung’s 7.1%. Its top three disclosed procurement counterparties represented 55% of procurement. [TF](https://www.trendforce.com/presscenter/news/20260312-12965.html), [AR25](https://investor.tsmc.com/sites/ir/annual-report/2025/2025%20TSMC%20Annual%20Report.E.pdf)

## 5. Overall Force Ratings

These are weighted judgments, not averages of the rows.

| Force | Overall pressure | Confidence | Main drivers | Trend |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| New entrants | Low | High | Capital requirements, process learning, switching costs, OIP ecosystem, expected retaliation | Stable |
| Suppliers | High | High | Sole-source EUV, limited qualified equipment/materials, high switching costs; partially offset by TSMC’s purchasing scale | Worsening |
| Buyers | Medium | High | Top-ten concentration of 78% is material, but differentiation, scarce capacity, and high switching costs limit leverage | Worsening |
| Substitutes | Low | Medium | Alternative fabs and nodes are imperfect; chiplets are a partial substitute that TSMC also supplies | Stable |
| Rivalry | Medium | High | High fixed costs, lumpy capacity, exit barriers, and national investment offset rapid growth and TSMC’s dominant share | Improving |

Supplier pressure receives a **High** rating even though supplier dependence is mutual: lack of an alternative to EUV and other qualified inputs is economically more important than the offsetting bargaining power from TSMC’s scale.

Rivalry remains **Medium**, despite TSMC’s share, because Intel, Samsung, and Chinese foundries may invest for strategic rather than purely financial reasons.

## 6. Most Important Forces

- **Most important:** Low threat of entry. It is the foundation of TSMC’s excess returns. Money can buy a fab, but not several generations of yield learning, production routines, ecosystem support, and customer trust.
- **Largest negative:** Supplier power. TSMC is highly dependent on irreplaceable equipment and specialized materials.
- **Changing fastest:** Rivalry is currently improving for TSMC. Its share expanded, industry demand grew 26.3% in 2025, and its margin widened while Intel continued to lose money.
- **Most misunderstood:** Buyer power. The 78% top-ten concentration looks dangerous, but advanced-chip buyers cannot cheaply move a qualified design when yield, launch timing, and power efficiency matter.
- **Profit protection:** The core profit pool is structurally protected. However, Q2 2026’s 67.7% gross margin is probably not entirely structural; favorable utilization, product mix, node scarcity, and currency can normalize.

## 7. Stock-Relevant Takeaways

- **Pricing power:** Strong at advanced nodes and advanced packaging. Higher ASPs alongside rising volumes and margins are evidence of value-based pricing.
- **Margin durability:** The five-year gross-margin average of 56.3% is more defensible than extrapolating the latest 67.7% quarter. Durable advantages should keep TSMC above most manufacturing peers, but node ramps and utilization still matter.
- **Capital intensity:** Extremely high. Capex was 33.4% of 2025 revenue and the 2026 budget is US$52–56 billion. High returns depend on disciplined capacity planning.
- **Growth quality:** Strong because advanced computing demand is expanding rather than merely shifting market share. The weakness is concentration: HPC represented 58% of 2025 revenue and 66% in Q2 2026.
- **Main risks:** AI overinvestment and underutilization; major-customer concentration; Taiwan geopolitical disruption; ASML/material bottlenecks; N2/A16 yield delays; overseas-fab cost dilution; export controls; and subsidized excess capacity.
- **Evidence that would change the conclusion:**
  - Sustained loss of foundry share or major advanced-node customer dual-sourcing.
  - Gross margin falling below the five-year average while utilization remains high.
  - Samsung or Intel demonstrating comparable yield, scale, and external-customer adoption.
  - Repeated N2/A16 delays or advanced-packaging shortages.
  - AI demand weakening enough to create prolonged idle capacity.
  - Supplier price increases materially exceeding TSMC’s ability to pass costs through.

## 8. Final Synthesis

Overall industry structure is **mixed**: low entry and substitution threats protect the profit pool, but high supplier power and capital-driven rivalry make the industry unattractive for subscale firms. TSMC is much better positioned than peers against nearly every force.

| Force | Overall pressure | Trend | Why it matters for the stock |
|---|---|---|---|
| New entrants | Low | Stable | Protects TSMC’s pricing, utilization, and long-term returns. |
| Suppliers | High | Worsening | Tool scarcity and rising process complexity can absorb part of TSMC’s economics and constrain growth. |
| Buyers | Medium | Worsening | Concentration raises demand volatility, but switching costs preserve pricing leverage. |
| Substitutes | Low | Stable | Alternative fabs remain imperfect; packaging substitutes are partly captured inside TSMC. |
| Rivalry | Medium | Improving | TSMC is widening its lead, but state-backed capacity keeps long-cycle oversupply risk alive. |

## 9. Mistakes-to-Avoid Check

| Common mistake | Avoided? | How |
|---|---|---|
| Define competition only as direct competitors | Yes | Included IDMs, in-house production, older nodes, packaging, and architectural substitutes. |
| Define the industry too broadly or narrowly | Yes | Focused on global leading-edge foundry while retaining mature-node effects where relevant. |
| Make lists without explaining causes | Yes | Each sub-point includes numerical evidence and an economic interpretation. |
| Give every force equal weight | Yes | Entry barriers and supplier power received greater weight. |
| Confuse effects with causes | Yes | Buyer sensitivity, margins, and rivalry were tied to concentration, switching costs, utilization, and fixed costs. |
| Assume fast growth makes the industry attractive | Yes | Capital intensity and supplier/rivalry risks remain despite strong growth. |
| Assume advanced technology makes it attractive | Yes | Technology is valuable only when paired with yield, scale, ecosystem, and customer trust. |
| Treat government as a sixth force | Yes | Policy was incorporated into entry barriers, supplier access, and rivalry. |
| Treat complements as a sixth force | Yes | EDA/IP and packaging were analyzed through entry barriers, switching costs, substitutes, and rivalry. |
| Rely on one-year results | Yes | Used five completed fiscal years and treated Q2 2026 only as a current indicator. |
| Assume consolidation fixes rivalry | Yes | High exit barriers and state-backed investment remain. |
| Use the framework only to label the industry good/bad | Yes | Connected the forces to pricing, margins, reinvestment, growth quality, and risk. |
