# LVMH — Porter Five Forces Stock Analysis

Prepared using [`SKILL.md`](../SKILL.md).

**Data cutoff:** August 11, 2026. The latest completed fiscal year is FY2025; the latest trading update used is LVMH's H1 2026 result, published July 27, 2026. This is an industry-structure analysis, not a valuation or buy/sell recommendation.

## Executive conclusion

LVMH operates in a **mixed but structurally attractive** industry. Entry at small scale is easy, but building a global high-luxury Maison is hard: heritage, controlled distribution, creative and craft capability, prime locations, inventory, and years of brand investment cannot be bought quickly. LVMH is better protected than most peers because it combines 75 Maisons, a 6,283-store network, vertical integration, Sephora, and €9.2 billion of annual communication spend.

The protection is not absolute. Luxury customers have almost no contractual switching costs, personal-luxury demand contracted in 2024-2025, and resale plus luxury experiences are improving substitutes. The result is a winner-takes-most profit pool: Hermès continued to grow at a 41% operating margin while Kering's 2025 margin fell to 11.1%. LVMH sits between them. Its Fashion and Leather Goods margin remained exceptional at 35.0% in 2025, but Group gross margin and the ROIC proxy fell from the 2021-2023 peak.

**Weighted force ratings:** New entrants **Low** pressure; Suppliers **Medium**; Buyers **Medium**; Substitutes **High**; Rivalry **Medium**. The controlling economic force is rivalry for brand desirability, increasingly amplified by substitutes and buyer scrutiny of price versus value.

## 1. Company Snapshot

- **Company and ticker:** LVMH Moët Hennessy Louis Vuitton SE; **MC** on Euronext Paris. A US ADR trades as **LVMUY**.
- **What it does:** LVMH owns and develops luxury brands across wines and spirits, fashion and leather goods, perfumes and cosmetics, watches and jewelry, selective retailing, and hospitality/media/other activities. It had **75 Maisons, 211,552 employees, and 6,283 stores** at year-end 2025. [LVMH 2025 Universal Registration Document](https://lvmh-com.cdn.prismic.io/lvmh-com/aczo-pGXnQHGZKQ5_UniversalRegistrationDocument2025.pdf)
- **2025 revenue mix:** Wines and Spirits €5.36bn/**7%**; Fashion and Leather Goods €37.77bn/**47%**; Perfumes and Cosmetics €8.17bn/**10%**; Watches and Jewelry €10.49bn/**13%**; Selective Retailing €18.35bn/**23%**; other/eliminations about **1%**. Fashion and Leather Goods generated €13.21bn, or about **74% of Group recurring operating profit**, so it receives the greatest weight in the force ratings. [LVMH FY2025 results](https://www.lvmh.com/en/publications/solid-performance-in-a-disrupted-global-economic-and-geopolitical-environment)
- **Geography:** 2025 revenue was France **8%**, Europe excluding France **18%**, United States **26%**, Japan **8%**, Asia excluding Japan **26%**, and other markets **14%**. No single region dominates, but Asia including Japan was 34%. [LVMH 2025 URD](https://lvmh-com.cdn.prismic.io/lvmh-com/aczo-pGXnQHGZKQ5_UniversalRegistrationDocument2025.pdf)
- **Main revenue drivers:** brand desirability; traffic and conversion in owned stores and e-commerce; price/mix; new products and creative directors; tourism and local luxury demand; Champagne/cognac volumes; jewelry icons; Sephora traffic and assortment.
- **Buyer/counterparty mix by segment:** Fashion and Leather Goods was **95% retail/5% wholesale** in 2023-2025, a strong proxy for end-consumer fragmentation. Wines and Spirits primarily reaches consumers through wholesale/distribution and hospitality; **21% of Champagne and cognac sales** used Moët Hennessy-Diageo joint-venture distribution in 2025. Perfumes and Cosmetics sells mainly through selective retailers plus over **700 owned stores**, but the exact split was not found in public filings. Watches and Jewelry uses **969 owned stores** plus selected multi-brand retailers; the revenue split was not found. Selective Retailing sells to end consumers; Sephora had **2,242 stores in 35 countries**, while DFS's city-center Gallerias were about two-thirds of DFS revenue. [LVMH 2025 URD](https://lvmh-com.cdn.prismic.io/lvmh-com/aczo-pGXnQHGZKQ5_UniversalRegistrationDocument2025.pdf)
- **Supplier/seller mix by segment:** LVMH-owned vineyards supply **20%** of its annual Champagne grape needs; external growers supply the majority, with the largest supplier below 10%. Hennessy self-supplies less than 1% and buys from about **1,600 independent producers**. Louis Vuitton makes most leather goods in 29 workshops; its largest leather and metal-parts suppliers represent about **22% and 25%**, respectively. Six French beauty plants meet almost all needs of four major French beauty Maisons, but raw materials and packaging remain external and sometimes limited-source. Watches and Jewelry assembles a substantial proportion internally and uses diversified subcontractors. Exact supplier proportions for Selective Retailing were **not found in public filings**; assortment, airport concessions, prime leases, labor, and technology are the main supplier groups. [LVMH 2025 URD](https://lvmh-com.cdn.prismic.io/lvmh-com/aczo-pGXnQHGZKQ5_UniversalRegistrationDocument2025.pdf)
- **Main cost drivers:** 2025 cost of sales €27.28bn/**33.8% of revenue**; marketing and selling €29.91bn/**37.0%**; general and administrative €5.93bn/**7.3%**. Embedded across these lines were advertising/promotion of €9.21bn/**11.4%**, personnel cost of €15.22bn/**18.8%**, and total lease expense of €6.47bn/**8.0%**. Operating investment was €4.57bn/**5.7%**. [LVMH 2025 URD](https://lvmh-com.cdn.prismic.io/lvmh-com/aczo-pGXnQHGZKQ5_UniversalRegistrationDocument2025.pdf)

## 2. Relevant Industry Definition

**Product scope.** The core industry is **global branded personal luxury goods**: high-end leather goods, apparel, footwear, jewelry, watches, fragrances, cosmetics, and accessories. LVMH also competes in premium wines and spirits and in selective beauty/travel retail. This is more precise than treating all consumer goods as the industry, but it recognizes that LVMH is a portfolio rather than a single-product firm.

**Geographic scope.** Global. Brand creation can be local, but demand, tourist flows, media, prime retail locations, talent, raw materials, and capital are internationally contested.

**Economic weighting.** The overall judgments are weighted toward Fashion and Leather Goods because it was 47% of revenue and about 74% of recurring operating profit in 2025. Segment exceptions are stated where wines/spirits, beauty, watches/jewelry, or retail have different economics.

**Market size and trajectory.** Bain-Altagamma estimated the personal-luxury-goods market at **€358bn in 2025**, down 2% at current exchange rates after a second year of contraction, and forecast **2%-4% growth to €365bn-€373bn in 2026**. Luxury experiences continued to outgrow tangible goods. [Altagamma-Bain June 2026 update](https://altagamma.it/en/studi-e-ricerche/)

**Main competitors by arena:**

- Multi-category/high luxury: Hermès, Chanel, Richemont, Kering, Prada, Moncler, Burberry.
- Leather/fashion: Hermès, Chanel, Gucci, Saint Laurent, Bottega Veneta, Prada, Moncler, Burberry and independent designer labels.
- Watches/jewelry: Richemont's Cartier and Van Cleef & Arpels, Rolex, Swatch Group, independent Swiss brands, and branded/fine jewelers.
- Beauty: L'Oréal Luxe, Estée Lauder, Coty, Puig, Chanel, Shiseido, niche brands, and celebrity brands.
- Wines/spirits: Diageo, Pernod Ricard, Rémy Cointreau, Campari, privately held Champagne houses, and premium wine/spirits estates.
- Selective retail: Ulta Beauty, Douglas, department stores, brand websites/stores, Avolta, China Tourism Group Duty Free, and online platforms.

**Main substitutes:** pre-owned/resale luxury; premium non-luxury or accessible luxury; renting; counterfeit goods; experiences such as travel and hospitality; smartwatches for traditional watches; unbranded/niche beauty; and postponing the purchase entirely.

**Supplier/seller groups:** growers and agricultural producers; tanneries, textile mills, metal-parts makers, gemstone/diamond/gold suppliers; packaging and glass; artisans, designers, creative directors and retail labor; landlords and airport concession owners; media/influencers; logistics, technology and payment vendors; and third-party brands supplying Sephora/DFS.

**Buyer groups:** fragmented end consumers are the majority for owned retail; wholesale distributors and hospitality accounts matter most in Wines and Spirits; selective retailers matter in beauty; multi-brand dealers matter in watches; airport authorities, tour operators and brand partners are key counterparties for DFS. The exact Group-wide channel split was **not found in public filings**; the segment channel and store proxies in the snapshot are the best public approximation.

## 5. Overall Force Ratings

| Force | Overall pressure | Confidence | Main drivers | Trend |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| New entrants | **Low** | High | Capital requirements, hard-to-copy heritage/capability, controlled distribution, scale economies, and credible retaliation outweigh low customer switching costs. | Stable |
| Suppliers | **Medium** | High | Fragmented sourcing and vertical integration offset scarce appellation inputs, materials, artisans, creative talent, and prime locations. | Worsening |
| Buyers | **Medium** | High | Fragmented end buyers and differentiation reduce negotiation power, but switching is easy and aspirational clients have become more price/value sensitive. | Worsening |
| Substitutes | **High** | Medium | Resale, accessible premium, experiences, smartwatches, and postponement are easy to adopt and are improving faster than first-hand luxury goods. | Worsening |
| Rivalry | **Medium** | High | Slow growth, high sunk/fixed investment, brand share shifts, and heavy non-price spending are offset by differentiation and avoidance of direct price wars. | Worsening |

**Weighted judgment, not a simple average:** entry barriers are the foundation of the profit pool, but they do not guarantee that every incumbent earns high returns. Once inside, rivalry for cultural relevance and the buyer's discretionary wallet determines who captures the pool. That is why Hermès can earn a 41% margin while Kering earns 11.1%. Substitution receives a High rating because resale and experiences are both growing and switching is frictionless, but LVMH's leading icons and diversified portfolio make it better positioned than the average Maison.

## 6. Most Important Forces

- **Which force matters most?** Rivalry for brand desirability. Competition is not mainly a handbag price war; it is a recurring contest over product, creative talent, cultural relevance, scarcity, flagship experience, clienteling, and media attention. Those inputs explain both LVMH's €9.2bn communication bill and the wide profit dispersion across peers.
- **Which force is changing fastest?** Substitutes, followed closely by buyer price sensitivity. Resale reached about €50bn in 2025 and nearly doubled since 2019, while Bain says experiences continue to outgrow goods. Buyers lost during the price-elevation cycle can now move to used, premium, niche, travel, or no purchase with little friction.
- **Which force is most misunderstood by investors?** Buyer power. Customers are fragmented and cannot negotiate, which looks favorable, but they exercise power by silently switching or disappearing. The 60m-70m decline in active buyers over two years is more important than formal concentration.
- **Are current profits protected, temporary, or likely to be competed away?** The profit base of Louis Vuitton, Dior, Tiffany, Bvlgari, Sephora and other strong Maisons is structurally protected. The extraordinary 2021-2023 margin/ROIC level was partly temporary. LVMH's 2025 F&LG margin of 35% remains exceptional, but the fall from 39.9% in 2023 and lower Group ROIC show that excess returns still require continual reinvestment.

## 7. Stock-Relevant Takeaways

- **Pricing power:** Strong but increasingly conditional on product value and creativity. The next leg of healthy growth should come from volume, mix, icons, and client acquisition rather than price alone. H1 2026 was encouraging: Q2 organic growth returned in Fashion and Leather Goods, with strong jewelry and Sephora growth. [LVMH H1 2026](https://www.lvmh.com/en/publications/accelerating-growth-in-the-second-quarter---solid-first-half-results)
- **Margin durability:** Better than the average consumer company, but below the 2021-2023 peak unless demand and mix recover. Group gross margin fell from 68.8% in 2023 to 66.2% in 2025; F&LG still earned 35.0%. H1 2026 Group operating margin held at 22.5%.
- **Capital intensity:** Economically significant. 2025 required €4.57bn of operating investment, €6.47bn of lease expense, €9.21bn of communication, and €22.66bn of inventory. This is not a capital-light royalty model. It still produced €11.33bn of operating free cash flow, showing the quality of the profit pool.
- **Growth quality:** Best when local demand, volumes, new clients, iconic lines, and store productivity grow. It is lower quality when growth comes mainly from price, tourist currency arbitrage, acquisitions, or new square footage. The diversity of jewelry and Sephora partly offsets fashion/cognac volatility.
- **Company position versus peers:** Better than most against new entrants and distribution; better than smaller houses against suppliers; strong but uneven against buyers/substitutes; and advantaged in rivalry by scale and portfolio. Recent growth/margins lag Hermès and Richemont's jewelry engine but are much stronger than Kering's. Peer sources: [Hermès key figures](https://finance.hermes.com/en/key-figures/), [Richemont FY2026](https://www.richemont.com/news-media/press-releases-news/richemont-delivers-strong-sales-growth-and-solid-results-for-the-year-ended-31-march-2026/), [Kering 2025](https://www.kering.com/en/finance/about-kering/), [Chanel 2025](https://www.chanel.com/puls-img/1779118002743-fy25-results-press-release-en-final.pdf).
- **Main risks:** prolonged weakness or creative misses at Louis Vuitton/Dior; greater price/value resistance; China/tourism/currency exposure; gold, agricultural, craft-labor and rent inflation; tariffs and trade measures; resale/experiences taking wallet share; inventory impairments; and dependence on F&LG for about 74% of Group recurring profit.
- **Evidence that would improve the conclusion:** sustained F&LG organic growth above the market; stabilization or recovery in its 35% margin; Group gross margin moving back above 67%; ROIC proxy returning above 20%; client-base recovery without renewed price escalation; and growth across smaller Maisons rather than only a few icons.
- **Evidence that would weaken the conclusion:** another two years of buyer-base contraction; resale continuing to grow several times faster than new luxury; F&LG margin falling below the low-30s despite revenue recovery; advertising/capex rising faster than sales; material loss of market share at Louis Vuitton/Dior/Hennessy; or supplier inflation that cannot be passed through.

## 8. Mistakes to Avoid Check

| Common mistake | Avoided? | How |
|---|---|---|
| Define competition only as direct competitors | Yes | Included resale, experiences, accessible premium, smartwatches, counterfeits, renting and doing nothing. |
| Define the industry too broadly or too narrowly | Yes | Defined global personal luxury goods, then treated W&S and selective retail as segment exceptions. |
| Make lists without explaining causes using numbers | Yes | Each required row links evidence/statistics to profitability pressure. |
| Give equal weight to all forces | Yes | Weighted F&LG heavily because it is 47% of revenue and ~74% of recurring profit; emphasized rivalry/substitutes. |
| Confuse effect with cause | Yes | Buyer sensitivity is tied to low switching cost, discretionary ROI, price/value mismatch, and aspirational-income pressure. |
| Assume fast growth means attractive | Yes | Industry contracted in 2024-2025; protection is based on barriers/differentiation, not growth alone. |
| Assume advanced technology means attractive | Yes | Digital technology is treated mainly as an enabler of resale, discovery, and retail capability. |
| Treat government as a sixth force | Yes | Regulation is analyzed within entry, supplier, substitute, and rivalry economics. |
| Treat complements as a sixth force | Yes | Sephora, distributors, tourism, platforms and cultural partnerships are included under channels, buyers, suppliers, or rivalry. |
| Rely on one-year results | Yes | Used FY2021-FY2025 profitability, the 2023-2025 market cycle, and H1 2026 updates. |
| Assume consolidation fixes rivalry | Yes | LVMH's scale helps, but brand-level rivals remain strong and profit dispersion is wide. |
| Use the framework only to label the industry good/bad | Yes | Connected structure to pricing, margins, capital intensity, growth quality, strategic reinvestment, and disconfirming evidence. |

## Final Summary Table

| Force | Overall pressure | Trend | Why it matters for the stock |
|---|---|---|---|
| New entrants | **Low** | Stable | Durable heritage, controlled distribution, craft capability, inventory and spending protect leading Maisons and their pricing. |
| Suppliers | **Medium** | Worsening | Scarce materials, artisans, creative talent and locations can pressure gross margin, though scale and vertical integration soften the hit. |
| Buyers | **Medium** | Worsening | Buyers cannot negotiate but can switch or disappear; value perception now limits price-only growth. |
| Substitutes | **High** | Worsening | Resale and experiences are growing faster and can divert entry/aspirational spend, although iconic LVMH products retain relative strength. |
| Rivalry | **Medium** | Worsening | Slow growth and high non-price investment widen the gap between winning and losing brands; LVMH must keep reinvesting to defend excess returns. |
